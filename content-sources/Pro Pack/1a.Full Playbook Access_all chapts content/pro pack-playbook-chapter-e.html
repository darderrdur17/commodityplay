E.1
How a Trader Thinks
The mental model behind every commercial decision — thesis, exit, downside.
10 min
Starter
Section E.1 · Chapter E
A trade idea and a trade are two very different things. Most people in and around commodity markets have plenty of trade ideas. The discipline is in knowing which ones to act on — and how.

Before entering any position, an experienced commodity trader asks three questions. First: What is my thesis? Not 'I think crude will go up' — that is not a thesis. A thesis is a specific causal argument: EIA data will show three consecutive bullish draws next month because refinery utilisation is rising faster than import volumes are replacing the Libyan production shortfall. A good thesis names the cause, the mechanism, and the expected market response.

Second: What is my exit? Every position should have a defined exit point before it is entered — both the target (the price level at which the thesis is proven and profits are taken) and the stop (the price level at which the thesis is disproven and losses are cut). A position without an exit plan will inevitably be held too long on the losing side and exited too early on the winning side, because human psychology pushes in both directions simultaneously.

Third: What is my downside? The trader calculates the maximum loss they would accept if the thesis is wrong — and sizes the position so that this loss is within their risk limits and their own tolerance for being wrong. A high-conviction trade in a liquid market with a clear stop might justify a full position. A speculative view in a thin market without a clear catalyst might justify 20% of that position.

The three questions are not bureaucratic. They are the difference between trading and gambling. Gambling is taking a position because you feel like it might work. Trading is taking a position because you have a thesis, an exit, and a clearly defined maximum loss.

What this means for you

These three questions apply to any commercial decision — not just futures trades. Before presenting a market view, a deal structure, or an operational recommendation, ask yourself: what is my thesis, what would disprove it, and what is the maximum cost if I am wrong? Structuring your thinking this way makes you significantly more persuasive and more rigorous simultaneously.

E.2
The Arbitrage Window
How regional price gaps open, close, and get exploited in physical commodity markets.
12 min
Starter
Section E.2 · Chapter E
The arbitrage window is the physical commodity market's most time-sensitive opportunity. When a price gap between two markets exceeds the cost of bridging them, money flows toward the higher-priced market until the gap closes. The desk that moves first captures the margin. The desk that moves second finds the window already closed.

An arbitrage window opens when the price differential between two regional markets for the same commodity exceeds the total cost of moving a cargo from the lower-priced market to the higher-priced one. The calculation is straightforward in principle: Gross spread − freight − port costs − boil-off − credit margin = net arb margin. A positive net arb margin means the window is open; a negative margin means it is not viable.

The forces that open a window are the same forces that close it — because exploiting the arb is what closes it. When Atlantic Basin LNG desks divert cargoes toward Asia to capture a wide JKM/TTF spread, those additional cargoes increase supply in Asia (pushing JKM down) and reduce supply in Europe (pushing TTF up). Simultaneously, the increased demand for LNG carriers on the long Asia route pushes freight rates higher — further compressing the net arb margin. The window closes not because the fundamental imbalance resolves, but because the act of exploiting it resolves the imbalance.

Speed of execution is the primary determinant of whether a desk captures an arb or watches it close. The four capabilities that determine execution speed are: freight access (vessel availability and pre-fixed rates), counterparty network (a buyer at the destination who can commit quickly), contractual flexibility (DES contracts with destination flexibility or spot cargoes with no destination restriction), and internal approval speed (the ability to get commercial approval without bureaucratic delay).

The arb window is not a gift. It is a race. By the time most of the market knows it's open, the fastest desks are already fixing vessels. Speed and preparation — not analysis after the fact — determine who captures the margin.

What this means for you

Understanding the arbitrage mechanism is the single most direct answer to the interview question 'how do commodity trading desks make money?' Being able to explain — with a specific example and worked numbers — how a regional price gap opens, how it is calculated, and why it closes naturally demonstrates commercial intelligence in a way that generic answers about supply and demand do not.

E.3
The Cargo Diversion Decision Tree
The full commercial logic for rerouting a vessel mid-voyage — with a downloadable decision framework.
11 min
Starter
Section E.3 · Chapter E
A cargo diversion is the most compressed commercial decision in physical commodity trading. In the time it takes to make the call — hours, sometimes less — the spread may have already narrowed, the freight rates may have moved, and the best counterparty may have already committed to another desk.

The cargo diversion decision begins with a spread calculation — but the spread alone is not sufficient to make the call. The desk must work through a sequence of questions simultaneously: Is the spread wide enough after all costs? Is a suitable vessel available at the assumed freight rate? Does the contractual structure of the cargo allow diversion — is it DES with destination flexibility, or FOB where the original buyer controls the vessel? If the original buyer must release the cargo, what is their compensation demand?

The second layer of the decision covers operational feasibility. Can the vessel reach the new destination within the available loading or delivery window? Is the receiving terminal at the new destination available and capable of accepting the cargo — correct draft, regasification capacity, product specification compatibility? Is the counterparty at the new destination creditworthy and able to commit immediately? Each question must be answered before committing to the diversion — because reversing a diversion decision mid-execution is extremely costly.

The third layer covers risk management. What is the P&L sensitivity to the spread narrowing by 20% after the decision is made? What happens to the position if freight rates move further against the desk after the vessel is re-fixed? Is the diversion approved within the desk's position limits and VaR framework? A diversion that looks compelling on a base-case calculation may be unacceptable once stress scenarios are applied — and the risk manager's involvement in this assessment is not optional.

The diversion decision is not one question — it's twelve questions, asked in parallel, with a clock running. The desks that answer them fastest and most completely are the ones that capture the margin. Preparation — mapping the answers to each question before the market moves — is the only way to be fast enough.

What this means for you

The cargo diversion decision tree is one of the most comprehensive commercial decision frameworks in commodity trading — and demonstrating that you understand every question in it is one of the strongest signals of commercial intelligence you can send in any interview for a trading, origination, or analytics role.

E.4
Timing a Hedge
How desks manage price risk between when a cargo is booked and when it is delivered.
11 min
Starter
Section E.4 · Chapter E
The hedge decision is not binary — it is continuous. The question is not whether to hedge but when, how much, and at what price. Getting all three right is the edge that separates the best risk managers from the rest.

When a physical trader buys a cargo, they immediately carry price risk — the risk that the commodity price falls before they sell it. They can hedge that risk by selling futures or swaps. But the decision of when to hedge is commercially significant. A trader who hedges immediately locks in the current price and removes all subsequent risk — but also removes all subsequent upside. A trader who delays hedging retains price exposure, hoping to sell the hedge at a better level — but risks the price moving further against them.

The hedge ratio — what percentage of the physical position is hedged with paper — is equally important. A 100% hedge is fully protected but captures no upside. A 50% hedge retains significant exposure. Mandatory hedge ratios enforced by risk policy remove trader discretion; desks with more flexible risk mandates allow traders to choose their hedge ratio based on their market view and risk appetite. Some desks hedge in tranches — placing 40% of the hedge immediately, then adding to it as the market evolves.

Basis risk affects the effectiveness of every hedge. If a West African crude cargo is hedged with ICE Brent futures, the hedge is effective only to the extent that the WA differential to Dated Brent remains stable. If the differential widens — WA crude weakens relative to Brent — the physical cargo loses value more than the futures hedge gains. The trader is exposed to the differential move despite being 'hedged' on flat price. Managing basis risk requires either hedging the differential separately or accepting that a proportion of the position will always be unhedged.

Being perfectly hedged means giving up all your upside to remove your downside. Most desks don't want that — they want to be selectively hedged, protecting against the bad scenario while retaining some ability to benefit if the view is right.

What this means for you

Hedge timing and hedge ratio decisions are made by traders — but they are evaluated by risk managers, implemented by the back office, and their effectiveness is assessed by finance. Understanding the full cycle of a hedging decision — from commercial logic through to ETRM booking and P&L attribution — makes you more effective in any function around the desk.

E.5
The P&L Attribution Map
Where trading profit actually comes from — all six levers mapped to a real cargo.
12 min
Starter
Section E.5 · Chapter E
Most trading P&L reports say 'crude trading made $X million this month.' What they don't say is how much of that came from flat price calls, how much came from basis, how much came from freight, and how much came from optionality. The attribution is where the real commercial intelligence lives.

P&L attribution is the process of decomposing a trading desk's profit or loss into its component sources. For a physical commodity desk running multiple concurrent positions, the total P&L in any period is the sum of contributions from: flat price moves (the change in value due to the overall price level moving), time spread (value earned or lost from holding positions across different delivery dates), location differential (value from moving commodity between markets), quality differential (value from the grade premium or discount), freight (P&L from freight positions relative to market), and optionality (value from flexibility provisions in contracts or vessel charters).

On any given trade, some levers contribute positively and others negatively. A cargo that made money overall might have lost $500,000 on flat price (the market moved against the trader), gained $800,000 on location differential (the destination market was stronger than expected), and gained $300,000 on freight (rates were fixed below market). The net $600,000 profit came not from being right on the flat price — where the desk was wrong — but from being right on the basis and the freight.

Understanding P&L attribution matters for multiple reasons. Risk managers use it to assess which positions are generating alpha and which are just lucky flat price bets. Traders use it to evaluate their own edge — are they consistently making money on basis, or consistently losing on freight? Analysts use it to build better models — if freight is consistently misforecast, the model needs improvement. Attribution transforms a P&L number into a learning tool.

The P&L number tells you whether you made money. The attribution tells you why — and why matters enormously, because a desk that makes money for the wrong reasons will eventually make money for none.

What this means for you

P&L attribution is a back-office and risk function in most firms, but the commercial logic behind it is essential for analysts, traders, and anyone evaluating the performance of a trading strategy. Being able to look at a P&L report and ask 'which lever generated this?' demonstrates a level of commercial sophistication that most candidates cannot match.

E.6
VaR, Limits & Risk Management
How the desk measures and controls its combined market exposure in real time.
12 min
Starter
Section E.6 · Chapter E
A trading desk without risk limits is not a trading desk — it is a fund. The limits are not constraints on ambition. They are the infrastructure that allows ambition to persist through the inevitable periods when the market is wrong about what you're right about.

Value at Risk (VaR) is the industry-standard metric for measuring commodity trading desk market risk. It estimates the maximum loss a portfolio of positions could suffer over a defined time horizon (typically 1 day or 10 days) at a defined confidence level (typically 95% or 99%). A VaR of $8 million at 95% confidence over one day means the desk should not expect to lose more than $8 million on 95% of trading days — or roughly 19 out of every 20 trading days.

VaR is calculated daily using historical price data and the current position. As positions change and market volatility changes, VaR changes — a large position in a highly volatile market may generate the same VaR as a larger position in a less volatile one. Risk managers set VaR limits for the desk, individual traders, and specific commodities or strategies. When a position approaches a limit, the risk manager flags it and may require the trader to reduce exposure — regardless of whether the trader believes the position is correct.

Beyond VaR, desks manage position limits (maximum notional exposure in a commodity), stop-loss limits (the maximum daily or monthly loss before the desk must reduce positions), and stress test results (how the portfolio performs under severe but plausible scenarios — e.g., a $30/bbl crude price move over 5 days). Each of these limits serves a different purpose: VaR manages routine risk, stop-losses prevent loss escalation, and stress tests protect against tail events that VaR underestimates.

Risk management is not about preventing the desk from losing money. It is about ensuring that no single loss — however bad — can threaten the firm's ability to continue operating. The limit protects the institution, not the trader.

What this means for you

Risk management is a discipline in its own right — and a growing function in every commodity trading firm as regulatory requirements increase. Even if you are targeting a front-office role, understanding VaR, limits, and the risk manager's role demonstrates the commercial maturity that senior hiring managers value. And if you are targeting a risk or middle-office role, this section is your starting point for a much deeper specialisation.

E.7
When the Trade Goes Wrong
Stop-loss discipline, loss management, and how desks cut positions without emotional bias.
11 min
Starter
Section E.7 · Chapter E
Losing money is not the failure. Failing to cut a losing position when the thesis is disproven is the failure. The distinction between those two things is the most important discipline in commodity trading — and the one that most people, at every level of experience, consistently struggle with.

When a position moves against the desk, there are two possibilities: the thesis is wrong, or the thesis is early. A position that is wrong should be cut — the evidence has changed, the market is telling you something you didn't know, and holding on increases the loss without improving the odds of recovery. A position that is early may be worth holding — the thesis is still intact, the fundamental picture supports the view, and the market will eventually confirm it. The challenge is that under loss pressure, these two states feel identical — and human psychology consistently misclassifies wrong positions as early ones.

Stop-loss discipline is the practice of committing to a maximum loss on a position before entering it and cutting the position automatically when that loss is reached — without re-evaluating the thesis under the influence of the current loss. The stop-loss is not a prediction about what the market will do at that level; it is a commitment about how much of the thesis you are willing to fund before accepting that it is disproven. A trader who consistently moves their stop-loss further away from entry is not showing flexibility — they are demonstrating the inability to accept being wrong.

The communication of losses up the management chain is a distinct skill from loss management itself. A position that has moved significantly against the desk must be reported promptly, with a clear account of the current thesis status (wrong or early), the remaining exposure, and the plan for managing it. Traders who delay reporting losses — hoping the position will recover before they have to explain it — universally make the situation worse. Risk managers who discover losses that were not reported promptly lose trust in the trader permanently.

The stop-loss is not there to tell you when the market is wrong. It is there to tell you when you are. The willingness to act on that information, without flinching, is the single most important risk management discipline on any desk.

What this means for you

The psychology of loss and the discipline of cutting positions are relevant to every function that makes recommendations — not just traders. An analyst who does not revisit a view when the evidence changes, an originator who does not walk away from a deal when the economics deteriorate, a scheduler who does not flag an operational problem early enough — all exhibit the same failure mode as a trader who won't cut a losing position. The discipline is universal.

E.8
A Week on the Desk — Putting It All Together
A narrative case study of a complete trading week — connecting every skill from the Playbook to a live commercial scenario.
15 min
Starter
Section E.8 · Chapter E
Everything in the Playbook has been building to this. One week, one desk, one set of markets — and every commercial decision made in real time, with the information available at the time, not the benefit of hindsight.

The scenario is a crude oil trading desk at a mid-sized commodity trading firm. It is Monday morning. The desk carries a net long position of 3 million barrels of North Sea Brent crude against ICE futures hedges. The hedge ratio is 80% — 20% of the position is open to flat price. The prior week's EIA report showed a 2.1 million barrel crude draw against a consensus estimate of 0.9 million — a bullish surprise. Brent is trading at $81.50, up $1.20 from Friday's close. The forward curve is in modest backwardation — M1 at $81.50, M3 at $80.20. The desk also has a VLCC fixed at WS95 on the Middle East Gulf–Rotterdam route, loading next Thursday.

Monday: The desk reviews overnight developments — news of an unplanned shutdown at a North Sea production platform has reduced expected loadings by approximately 500,000 barrels over the next two weeks. The fundamental case for the long position strengthens. The trader increases the open position from 20% to 35% by buying back some of the ICE hedge. Risk management flags the increased VaR. The trader provides the thesis: supply disruption tightens the near-term physical balance; the backwardation should steepen. The risk manager approves within limits. Wednesday: EIA report. The desk's expectation was a 1.5 million barrel draw based on the production platform news and the prior week's trend. Actual: a 3.8 million barrel draw. Brent rallies $2.40 in 15 minutes. The trader increases the hedge from 65% to 85% — locking in the majority of the week's gain. The forward curve moves into stronger backwardation.

Thursday: The VLCC loads at Ras Tanura. During loading, the independent surveyor finds a quality issue — sulphur content marginally above specification. The scheduling team negotiates a small price adjustment with the Rotterdam receiver. The cargo is accepted; demurrage of 14 hours is accrued during the inspection delay. Friday: The position is reviewed. The open position is reduced to 15% ahead of the weekend. Net P&L for the week: $4.2 million. Attribution: $2.8M from flat price (backwardation trade worked); $0.9M from time spread (front of curve strengthened relative to back); -$0.3M from basis (North Sea physical/ICE differential widened); $0.8M from freight (WS95 fixture below current WS110 market); -$0.1M from demurrage (operationally avoidable).

A good week on the desk is not one where everything goes perfectly. It is one where the thesis was sound, the risk was controlled, the losses were small and understood, and the gains reflected genuine commercial skill — not luck.

What this means for you

The skills that determined the outcomes in each day of this week — view formation, hedge timing, supply disruption analysis, scheduling coordination, freight positioning, and P&L attribution — are the skills covered in all five chapters of this Playbook. If you can follow the commercial logic of each decision in this case study and articulate why each choice was made, you have achieved the core learning objective of the Full Playbook.

A.1
What Is Physical Commodity Trading?
The difference between physical and paper markets — why a cargo is not a futures contract.
10 min
Starter
Section A.1 · Chapter A
Most people think commodity trading is about predicting where prices go. The traders who actually make money know it's about something else entirely.

When a commodity trader executes a deal, they are not placing a bet on a price chart. They are arranging the physical movement of a real substance — a cargo of LNG on a vessel 400 nautical miles off Singapore, a parcel of crude already loaded at Ras Tanura, a batch of gasoil sitting in a tank farm in Rotterdam — from where it is to where it needs to be, at a price that makes the journey worthwhile.

This is physical trading. It involves a vessel, a loading port, a delivery window, a cargo specification, a counterparty who actually needs the molecules, and an operations team making sure the whole chain executes on time. The paper market — futures on ICE or NYMEX, swaps, options — exists alongside the physical market but is not the same thing. A paper position can be opened and closed in seconds. A physical cargo cannot.

The two markets are deeply connected — physical traders use paper instruments to hedge price exposure, and paper markets are anchored to physical price assessments — but the disciplines are different. A physical trader needs to understand freight, cargo specs, laytime, demurrage, and credit. Most major commodity firms do both, but the commercial logic of the desk starts with the physical.

Physical trading is logistics with a P&L attached. Every barrel has to be somewhere, on something, going somewhere else — and the gap between what it costs to move it and what someone will pay for it when it arrives is where the money is made.

What this means for you

Whether you are entering a trading role, an analytics function, or a scheduling position, understanding the physical market is the baseline. The desk measures everything against physical reality — cargo availability, vessel positions, terminal throughput. Speak that language and you can follow the commercial logic behind every desk decision.

A.2
The Energy Markets Landscape
Oil, LNG, natural gas, and power — how each market is structured and how they connect.
12 min
Starter
Section A.2 · Chapter A
Commodity trading is not one industry. It is four overlapping industries that share infrastructure, pricing signals, and often the same desk.

The energy commodity market is commonly discussed as if it were a single entity — but in practice it is four distinct markets, each with its own pricing structure, physical infrastructure, and commercial logic. Crude oil and refined products form the largest and most liquid market. Crude is extracted, shipped to refineries, and converted into gasoline, gasoil, jet fuel, fuel oil, and naphtha. Each product has its own market, benchmark, and demand drivers.

LNG — liquefied natural gas — is the fastest-growing segment. Natural gas is supercooled to liquid at -162°C, loaded onto specialised carriers, and shipped globally. Key benchmarks are JKM (Japan Korea Marker) for Asian spot prices and TTF for European gas. The JKM/TTF spread determines whether Atlantic Basin cargoes flow east or stay in Europe — one of the most important arbitrage signals in commodity markets today.

Pipeline gas is geographically constrained — it cannot be rerouted the way a ship can. Power markets sit at the end of the chain, converting primary energy into electricity. Each commodity feeds into the next: an Australian LNG export drop affects European TTF, which affects coal-to-gas switching, which affects CO2 prices. Learning to trace these connections is the commercial analyst's core skill.

Energy markets are not separate silos. When Australian LNG exports drop, it affects European TTF prices, which affects coal-to-gas switching in power, which affects carbon prices. Tracing these connections is what separates a market reader from a news reader.

What this means for you

When you read about a refinery outage, trace it to product supply, then crude demand, then freight for vessels heading there. When you read about a cold snap in Northeast Asia, trace it to LNG demand, JKM prices, then Atlantic cargo flows. The market is always a chain of connected reactions.

Now that you can see the markets — how does a trading desk actually make money across all of them?

A.3
How a Trade Makes Money
The six revenue levers a commodity trader pulls — flat price, time spread, location, quality, freight, optionality.
11 min
Starter
Section A.3 · Chapter A
Most people assume commodity trading profit comes from buying low and selling high on flat price. Most commodity trading profit does not come from that at all.

Flat price — the outright price of crude or a product — is one source of revenue. But for most physical commodity desks, it is not the primary one. The reason is simple: flat price is the most competed, most watched signal in the market. Everyone has a view on where Brent is going. The real commercial edge comes from signals that are harder to read, faster to act on, and less efficiently priced.

There are six distinct levers a physical desk pulls. Flat price: buying low, selling high — the simplest but most competed. Time spread: exploiting price differences between delivery dates. A contango market rewards storage; backwardation rewards selling promptly. Location differential: the price gap between two regions. Moving a cargo from where it is cheap to where it is expensive, if freight allows.

Quality differential: different crude grades and product specs trade at different prices. Sourcing the right grade at the right differential for a specific refinery is a skill in itself. Freight: locking in cheap shipping before the market tightens generates P&L from the differential between what you pay and what the market moves to. Optionality: a cargo with flexible loading window or destination flexibility is worth more. Desks that structure optionality into positions earn when conditions change.

The best trades are the ones where you are right on three levers at once — the location arb is open, the time spread is working in your favour, and you have the freight locked before anyone else. That is when you make real money.

What this means for you

When you review a commodity trade or a market scenario, identify which of the six levers it is using — and which it is not. Most trades combine two or three levers simultaneously. Understanding which levers a desk is long or short on any given day is the foundation of commercial awareness that hiring managers test for.

A.4
Refinery Economics & Crack Spreads
Crude slate, product yields, and what the crack spread signals to the desk.
12 min
Starter
Section A.4 · Chapter A
A refinery does not just convert crude oil into products. It converts the price difference between crude and products into margin — and that margin tells the market something important every single day.

The refinery sits at the centre of the oil value chain. It buys crude at one price, processes it through distillation, cracking, and treating units, and sells the resulting products — gasoline, gasoil, jet fuel, fuel oil, naphtha — at a higher price. The difference between the revenue from products and the cost of crude input is the crack spread — the refinery's gross margin.

The standard benchmark is the 3-2-1 crack spread: three barrels of crude yield two barrels of gasoline and one barrel of distillate. The formula is (2 × gasoline price + 1 × distillate price − 3 × crude price) ÷ 3. At $95/bbl gasoline, $90/bbl distillate, and $75/bbl crude, the 3-2-1 crack is $18.33/bbl. After operating costs of roughly $5–8/bbl, the net refinery margin is around $10–13/bbl.

What makes crack spreads commercially important is what they signal. A widening crack signals strong product demand or tight crude supply — refiners are earning well and running hard. A compressing crack signals oversupply in products or a crude price spike that hasn't passed through to consumers yet. Refiners respond by adjusting run rates — which directly affects how much crude they buy and how much product hits the market.

The crack spread doesn't just tell you if refining is profitable — it tells you which direction the physical market is about to move. A refiner cutting runs today is tightening product supply in three weeks.

What this means for you

Check crack spreads before reading headline crude prices. If cracks are compressing while crude rallies, the rally may not hold — refiners will pull back demand. If cracks are widening while crude is flat, watch for product-led price action. The crack is the leading indicator; flat price often follows.

A.5
Shipping, Freight & Cargo Economics
Vessel classes, freight rate mechanics, laytime, and how freight changes the arbitrage calculus.
14 min
Starter
Section A.5 · Chapter A
Freight is not just a cost in commodity trading. It is a variable that can open or close an arbitrage window overnight — and a source of P&L in its own right.

Every physical commodity trade involves transportation. The economics of that transportation — vessel class, freight rate, route, speed, port costs — directly determine whether a trade is viable. A VLCC (Very Large Crude Carrier) carries 2 million barrels of crude and is used on long-haul routes like Middle East Gulf to Asia or the Americas. A Suezmax carries around 1 million barrels and is sized to transit the Suez Canal. An Aframax carries 500,000–750,000 barrels on shorter regional routes. LNG carriers are entirely different — purpose-built, insulated, and carrying between 125,000 and 210,000 cubic metres of liquefied gas.

Freight rates are quoted in Worldscale points for tankers — a percentage of a standard rate that accounts for vessel size and route — or in $/day for time charters. The Baltic Exchange publishes the key indices: the BDTI (Baltic Dirty Tanker Index) for crude tankers, the BCTI (Baltic Clean Tanker Index) for product tankers, and the BLNG for LNG carriers. When freight rates rise, the cost of moving a cargo increases — which narrows any arbitrage spread and may make a previously viable trade uneconomic.

Laytime is the time allowed for loading or discharging a cargo under the charter party. If the vessel exceeds this time, the charterer pays demurrage — typically $20,000–$60,000 per day for a VLCC. If loading completes early, the owner may pay dispatch. Laytime calculations depend on NOR (Notice of Readiness) tendering, port congestion, weather exceptions, and the specific charter party terms — and disputes over demurrage are extremely common.

The arbitrage calc is the easy part. The hard part is knowing whether the freight you need is actually available at the rate you assumed — and whether it will still be open by the time your cargo arrives.

What this means for you

Always run your freight assumption before committing to a physical trade. A 10% freight rate move on a VLCC changes the economics of a long-haul crude arbitrage by $0.30–$0.50/bbl. On a 2 million barrel cargo, that is $600,000–$1 million of P&L difference. Freight is never just a cost line.

A.6
Storage, Terminals & the Inventory Signal
What tank levels and floating storage tell a trader about supply balance.
11 min
Starter
Section A.6 · Chapter A
The EIA inventory report comes out every Wednesday at 10:30am New York time. What the desk looks for in those 90 seconds tells you everything about how physical traders actually read the market.

Physical commodity storage is the buffer between supply and demand. When supply exceeds demand, inventory builds — in tank farms, pipelines, and increasingly in vessels used as floating storage. When demand exceeds supply, inventories draw. The level and direction of inventory change is one of the most reliable signals of physical market balance available to a trader.

The EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report is released every Wednesday and covers US crude and product inventories — crude stocks, gasoline, distillates, and refinery inputs. The market watches the crude draw or build against analyst consensus. A draw that beats expectations — say, a 4.2 million barrel draw against a consensus estimate of 1.8 million barrels — is a bullish signal. But the desk also watches days of forward cover (how many days of consumption current stocks represent), refinery utilisation rates, and the split between commercial stocks and the Strategic Petroleum Reserve.

Floating storage — vessels anchored and used as storage rather than in transit — appears when the contango in the forward curve is large enough to cover vessel hire costs plus insurance and financing. During COVID in 2020, floating storage reached record levels as onshore tanks filled and the curve went into extreme contango. Tracking floating storage via AIS data gives traders a real-time read on supply balance that is not captured in any official inventory report.

The inventory signal is not the number — it is the number relative to the expectation, relative to the seasonal norm, and relative to what the price already moved in anticipation. All three together tell you whether the signal is real.

What this means for you

When reading an EIA report, never focus only on the headline crude number. Check the product split, the refinery utilisation rate, and the import/export flows. And always compare to the 5-year seasonal average — a 3 million barrel build in March is very different from the same build in August when seasonal patterns diverge sharply.

A.7
The Role of Price Reporting Agencies
How Platts and Argus assess prices — and why the last 30 minutes of the window moves markets.
10 min
Starter
Section A.7 · Chapter A
Every day at a specific time, a small team of price reporters at Platts and Argus publish benchmark prices that govern trillions of dollars of commodity contracts globally. Understanding how they do it explains something important about how physical commodity markets actually work.

Physical commodity prices are not set by an exchange. Unlike crude futures on ICE or NYMEX, which are traded continuously in an open market, physical prices for crude grades, refined products, and LNG are assessed by Price Reporting Agencies (PRAs) — primarily Platts (now S&P Global Commodity Insights), Argus Media, and ICIS. These assessments are then used as reference prices in billions of dollars of physical supply contracts and financial derivatives.

The dominant methodology is the Market on Close (MOC) process, used by Platts for crude and products. During a defined assessment window — typically the last 30 minutes of the trading day — reporters gather bids, offers, and transactions from market participants. They assess the price at which a cargo could be bought or sold at the close of that window, applying a set of published criteria to determine which submissions are credible. The result is a single assessed price — the benchmark.

This structure has significant commercial implications. Because the benchmark is set in the last 30 minutes, large market participants can and do influence assessments by placing strategic bids or offers during the window. This is not illegal — it is how the market works — but it means a trader who understands the MOC process can read assessment-day positioning as a market signal in itself. Understanding assessment timing, the methodology differences between Platts and Argus, and how benchmarks like Dated Brent or JKM are constructed is essential for anyone working near the front office.

Price reporters don't just record what the market does — they assess what they believe a fair transaction would look like, based on available evidence. That distinction matters enormously when the market is thin and a small number of participants are setting the reference price for millions of barrels.

What this means for you

If you work in scheduling, analytics, risk, or trade finance, you will encounter Platts and Argus assessments constantly — in contract pricing, hedge references, and settlement calculations. Understanding how those prices are constructed helps you identify when an assessment may not reflect true market conditions — and when that discrepancy is a commercial risk or opportunity.

A.8
Risk Management on the Desk
VaR, position limits, and how the desk manages market and credit exposure.
13 min
Starter
Section A.8 · Chapter A
Every trading desk operates inside a set of risk constraints it cannot breach. Understanding those constraints — how they are set, how they are monitored, and what happens when a position approaches a limit — is as important as understanding the trades themselves.

Value at Risk (VaR) is the most commonly used measure of market risk on a commodity trading desk. It estimates the maximum loss a portfolio of positions could suffer over a given time horizon at a given confidence level — typically 95% or 99% over a one-day or ten-day period. A VaR of $5 million at 95% confidence over one day means the desk should not expect to lose more than $5 million on more than 5% of trading days. VaR is used to set position limits — the maximum market exposure a trader or desk can carry — and is recalculated daily using current market data and the desk's live position.

Beyond VaR, desks manage several distinct risk types simultaneously. Price risk — the risk that flat price moves against the position — is the most obvious. Basis risk is the risk that the hedge and the physical position don't move together perfectly. Credit risk is the risk that a counterparty defaults before a trade settles. Operational risk covers vessel delays, cargo rejections, and documentation failures. Liquidity risk is the risk that a position cannot be exited at a reasonable price when needed.

Risk managers and traders interact daily on a commodity desk — and the relationship is not always comfortable. A risk manager's job is to enforce the limits and ask the questions a trader under P&L pressure might not want to answer. A trader's job is to take positions within those limits and explain their thesis clearly. When a position approaches a VaR limit, the risk manager can require the trader to reduce exposure — regardless of whether the trader believes the position is right. This separation of authority is what prevents single positions from becoming existential threats to the firm.

Risk management is not about preventing losses. It is about ensuring that no single loss, however bad, can threaten the desk's ability to keep trading. The limit exists so that being wrong once doesn't mean being out of business.

What this means for you

If you are targeting a risk, analytics, or middle-office role, this section is your starting point — but also read it if you are targeting a front-office role. Traders who understand the risk framework their desk operates within make better commercial decisions, because they know exactly how much latitude they have before the risk manager intervenes.

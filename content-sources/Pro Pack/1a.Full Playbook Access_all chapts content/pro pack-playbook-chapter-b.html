B.1
The Physical Trade Lifecycle
From cargo enquiry to final settlement — every step a physical deal goes through.
12 min
Starter
Section B.1 · Chapter B
A physical commodity trade is not a single transaction. It is a chain of at least a dozen separate steps — each of which can go wrong, each of which has commercial and legal implications, and each of which requires a different function of the desk to execute correctly.

The lifecycle of a physical commodity deal begins with an enquiry — a buyer or seller reaches out to the market to test appetite. This is followed by negotiation of the commercial terms: volume, quality specification, price formula, loading port, delivery window, and payment terms. Once terms are agreed, a confirmation is exchanged — a legally binding document that records every agreed term. For large LNG deals this confirmation may be 20–30 pages; for spot crude trades it may be a one-page recap.

Once confirmed, the deal enters the operations phase. The scheduling team manages the vessel nomination, coordinates with the terminal for a loading slot, arranges the bill of lading and other shipping documents, and monitors the cargo in transit. For a DES trade, the seller owns and manages the vessel through to delivery; for an FOB trade, the buyer takes responsibility from the loading port. The operations team tracks the vessel via AIS and manages any changes to the delivery window.

Settlement occurs after delivery — the final invoice is calculated using the pricing formula in the confirmation, the cargo quantity is confirmed via an independent surveyor's report, and payment is made within the agreed credit terms (typically 30 days). The deal then flows into the back office for P&L booking in the ETRM system — connecting the physical trade to the hedge positions that were placed alongside it.

Every step of the trade lifecycle is a potential failure point. The front office closes the deal in an hour. Operations manages it for three weeks. The desk that ignores the operational layer will eventually have a very expensive surprise.

What this means for you

If you are targeting a scheduling, operations, or ETRM role, map every step of this lifecycle to the function that executes it — front office, middle office, back office, legal, and credit. Understanding where handoffs occur and what can go wrong at each one is what separates an effective operations professional from one who just processes paperwork.

B.2
Futures, Swaps & Options
The paper instruments a desk uses alongside physical positions.
13 min
Starter
Section B.2 · Chapter B
Physical commodity traders are not just buying and selling cargoes. Every cargo they own carries a price risk that has to be managed — and the tools they use to manage it are futures, swaps, and options.

A futures contract is a standardised, exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell a specific commodity at a specific price on a specific date. WTI crude futures on NYMEX and Brent futures on ICE are the most actively traded commodity futures contracts in the world. Futures are used by physical traders to hedge price exposure — if you own a cargo of crude, you sell futures to lock in the current price and remove the risk that the price falls before you sell the cargo.

A swap is an over-the-counter (OTC) contract where two parties exchange cash flows based on the difference between a floating price and a fixed price. Unlike futures, swaps are customised — they can reference any benchmark, any volume, any time period. For LNG traders, a JKM swap allows them to fix the price of a cargo that will be priced against the JKM assessment at delivery. Swaps are bilateral and carry counterparty credit risk; futures are exchange-cleared and carry no credit risk to the counterparty.

Options give the buyer the right — but not the obligation — to buy or sell a commodity at a specific price. A cap protects against price rising above a level; a floor protects against it falling below. Options cost a premium upfront but preserve upside. Physical traders use options when they want asymmetric protection — hedging downside risk while keeping the ability to benefit if the market moves in their favour.

The physical cargo is the position. The futures or swap is the hedge. The P&L lives in the difference between what you locked in on the paper and what you actually sell the physical for. Understanding that relationship is the entire job.

What this means for you

Even if you are not targeting a trading role, understanding these instruments is essential. Schedulers manage positions that are partially hedged. Risk managers monitor the hedge ratio. Analysts build the curve that prices the swaps. Every function on a commodity desk touches these instruments in some way.

B.3
Hedging a Physical Position
How a desk removes price risk from a cargo using paper markets.
11 min
Starter
Section B.3 · Chapter B
A hedge is not a bet in the opposite direction. It is a deliberate decision to remove price risk from a position — and the discipline of when to hedge, how much to hedge, and when not to hedge is one of the most important commercial skills on the desk.

When a physical trader buys a cargo of crude at $78/bbl, they immediately face the risk that the price will fall before they sell it. To remove that risk, they sell futures or swaps at the same price — creating a hedge. If crude falls to $74/bbl, the physical cargo loses $4/bbl, but the short futures position gains $4/bbl. The losses and gains offset — the desk is flat the market. If crude rises to $82/bbl, the physical cargo gains $4/bbl but the futures position loses $4/bbl. Again, flat the market.

In practice, hedges are rarely perfect. Basis risk is the risk that the physical price and the futures price do not move together exactly. A cargo of West African crude priced against Dated Brent may be hedged with ICE Brent futures — but if the Brent-WA differential widens, the hedge loses effectiveness. The desk is still exposed to the differential move even though it thought it was hedged. Managing basis risk is a significant part of day-to-day risk management.

The decision of how much to hedge — the hedge ratio — is both commercial and strategic. A 100% hedge removes all price risk but also all upside. A 50% hedge retains some exposure to the market's direction. Some desks have mandatory hedge ratios enforced by risk policy; others allow trader discretion. The choice depends on the firm's risk appetite, the desk's P&L mandate, and the trader's confidence in their market view.

Being perfectly hedged means being perfectly wrong — you gave away all your upside to remove your downside. The art is knowing how much risk you actually need to carry to generate the return the business requires.

What this means for you

Every time you see a commodity firm report a trading loss, the question to ask is not 'why did the price go against them?' but 'why wasn't the position hedged?' The answer usually involves one of three things: deliberate directional exposure, basis risk that wasn't accounted for, or a hedge that worked in theory but couldn't be executed in the market at the assumed rate.

B.4
Basis & the Differential
Why the same commodity trades at different prices in different places.
10 min
Starter
Section B.4 · Chapter B
Dubai crude is not Brent crude. Dated Brent is not ICE Brent futures. HSFO in Singapore is not HSFO in Rotterdam. Every commodity trades at a spread to every benchmark — and understanding that spread is what separates a physical trader from a paper one.

Basis is the difference between the physical commodity price and the benchmark futures price. For a US Gulf Coast refiner buying domestic crude, the basis is the difference between the wellhead price of a specific crude grade and WTI futures. Basis reflects the cost of transportation to the delivery point of the futures contract, quality differences between the physical grade and the benchmark grade, and any supply/demand imbalances specific to that location.

In practice, traders talk about differentials — the premium or discount at which a physical commodity trades relative to a benchmark. Dubai crude trades at a differential to Brent. West African Bonny Light trades at a premium to Dated Brent because of its lower sulphur content and higher gasoline yield. Russian Urals trades at a discount because it is heavier and higher in sulphur. These differentials move constantly based on refinery demand, freight rates, and regional supply dynamics.

Basis risk arises when a hedge is placed using a benchmark that does not perfectly track the physical commodity being hedged. A Singapore gasoil trader who hedges a physical gasoil cargo with ICE gasoil futures is exposed to the Singapore-Rotterdam gasoil differential — the basis. If Singapore gasoil weakens relative to Rotterdam, the physical cargo loses value even though the futures hedge holds. Basis risk is real, difficult to eliminate entirely, and the source of many trading losses that are mistakenly attributed to 'being wrong on the market'.

Traders lose money on basis all the time while being right on flat price. You can nail the direction of Brent perfectly and still lose money on a West African cargo if you didn't account for the differential move.

What this means for you

When you are reading about commodity price differentials in the news — Urals at a discount to Brent, JKM premium to TTF narrowing — you are reading about basis. Understanding what drives those differentials (quality, freight, regional supply, sanctions) is what connects the financial market to the physical one.

B.5
The Exchange of Futures for Swaps (EFS)
How traders move between physical and paper positions.
9 min
Starter
Section B.5 · Chapter B
The EFS is one of the least-discussed mechanisms in commodity markets — and one of the most important for understanding how physical and paper markets stay connected to each other.

An Exchange of Futures for Swaps (EFS) is a privately negotiated transaction in which one party exchanges a futures position for an OTC swap position. The exchange takes place at an agreed price and is then registered with the exchange. The result is that one party ends up with a futures contract and the other ends up with a swap — each has moved from one instrument to the other without entering the open market.

EFS transactions are used when a trader wants to move from a standardised exchange-traded position to a customised OTC position — or vice versa. For example, a physical LNG trader who has hedged a cargo with JKM futures may want to convert that hedge into a JKM swap with a different settlement date to better match the physical cargo's pricing period. The EFS allows this conversion without the market impact of unwinding the futures position and placing a new swap separately.

EFS activity is a useful market intelligence signal. High EFS volume in a specific commodity and tenor suggests that physical market participants are actively adjusting their hedge positions — often because physical market conditions are changing. When a large volume of crude futures is exchanged into longer-dated swaps, it may signal that desks are repositioning for a change in the forward curve structure.

Every time the physical and paper markets seem to be telling different stories, look at the EFS volume. It's often where traders are quietly moving their positions from one world to the other.

What this means for you

For most students and career switchers, the EFS is a concept to understand rather than a mechanism to operate. Knowing what it is and why it exists helps you understand how the physical and paper markets remain connected — and how large desks manage their positions across both simultaneously.

B.6
Options in Commodity Markets
Caps, floors, collars — how optionality is priced and used.
12 min
Starter
Section B.6 · Chapter B
Options give you the right to act without the obligation. In commodity markets, that asymmetry — protecting against the bad outcome while keeping the good one — has real and measurable value.

A commodity call option gives the buyer the right to purchase a commodity at a fixed price (the strike price) before a specific date. A put option gives the right to sell at the strike price. The buyer pays a premium upfront for this right. If the market moves in the buyer's favour, they exercise the option and benefit. If the market moves against them, they let the option expire and only lose the premium — unlike a futures contract, where losses are uncapped.

In commodity markets, options are used in several common structures. A cap is a call option that protects a buyer against prices rising above a level — used by utilities and airlines to fix their maximum fuel cost. A floor is a put option that protects a seller against prices falling below a level — used by producers to guarantee a minimum revenue. A collar combines a cap and a floor — the buyer gives up some upside in exchange for paying a lower premium, or no premium at all if the collar is costless.

Option pricing is driven primarily by implied volatility — the market's expectation of how much the underlying commodity price will move. High volatility means options are expensive because the probability of the option being in-the-money is higher. Physical commodity traders watch implied volatility as a market signal: a sudden spike in crude option volatility often precedes or accompanies a significant physical market event — a supply disruption, an OPEC announcement, or a demand shock.

The premium you pay for an option is the price of sleeping well at night. The question is whether the cost of that peace of mind is justified by the risk you're actually carrying.

What this means for you

Options are used extensively in trade finance and hedging structures for commodity deals — particularly long-term LNG offtake agreements and producer hedging programmes. Understanding the basic mechanics — what a cap and floor do, what affects the premium, how optionality in a physical contract has measurable value — gives you a significant edge in analytical and structuring roles.

B.7
Term Contracts vs. Spot Trading
The commercial logic behind long-term offtake and spot market exposure.
10 min
Starter
Section B.7 · Chapter B
A commodity desk that trades only spot is taking maximum market exposure. One that trades only on long-term contracts is leaving significant upside on the table. The mix between term and spot is one of the most consequential strategic decisions a commodity firm makes.

A term contract is a multi-year supply agreement between a producer and a buyer — typically at a price linked to a benchmark with an agreed formula. LNG term contracts are the clearest example: a Japanese utility might sign a 20-year contract with an Australian LNG producer, with volume and price formula agreed upfront. Term contracts provide certainty for both parties — the producer finances the investment, the buyer secures supply. But they also remove the ability to benefit from spot market moves.

The spot market is where uncontracted volumes trade — cargo by cargo, at the current market price. Trading firms active in the spot market can capture arbitrage opportunities, respond to supply disruptions, and earn from volatility in ways that term-contract buyers cannot. But they also face the risk that spot prices move sharply against them, and they must continuously source supply and place cargoes without the certainty of contracted volumes.

Most large commodity traders and energy companies run a portfolio approach — anchoring their book with enough term contracts to guarantee base supply and revenue, then trading the margin on the spot market. The optimal ratio between term and spot exposure depends on the firm's risk tolerance, the volatility of the underlying commodity, and the desk's ability to manage spot market positions actively. A common structure for LNG is 70–80% term, 20–30% spot — but this shifts significantly during periods of high market volatility.

A term contract is certainty. A spot position is optionality. Most commodity firms need both — and the firms that manage the balance best are the ones that consistently outperform their peers.

What this means for you

If you are targeting an origination or commercial role, understanding the term vs. spot trade-off is central to how you will structure deals and advise clients. If you are targeting an analytics or scheduling role, understanding this distinction explains why some cargo programmes are fixed and others are dynamic — and why your scheduling flexibility changes depending on the contract type.

B.8
The Trading Book — How Positions Stack Up
How a desk tracks its combined physical, paper, and freight exposure.
11 min
Starter
Section B.8 · Chapter B
At any given moment, a commodity trading desk has dozens of positions simultaneously — physical cargoes on the water, futures hedges on the exchange, OTC swaps with counterparties, freight fixtures, and term contract obligations. The trading book is how the desk sees all of them at once.

The trading book is the consolidated view of all open positions on the desk — physical and paper, long and short, hedged and unhedged. It is maintained in an ETRM system (Energy Trading and Risk Management platform) — typically Endur, RightAngle, or Allegro — which books every trade, marks positions to market daily, calculates P&L, and feeds risk metrics to the risk management function.

A well-structured trading book shows, for every commodity and time period: the net physical long or short position (how many barrels or MMBtu the desk owns or owes), the hedge position (the offsetting paper contracts), the net unhedged exposure (the open position), and the current mark-to-market P&L on each leg. Traders review their book every morning before markets open — checking overnight price moves, assessing whether any positions need to be adjusted, and planning the day's trading activity.

The interaction between physical and paper positions in the book creates a number of important metrics that risk managers monitor daily. Hedge effectiveness — what percentage of the physical exposure is covered by paper hedges. Greeks — for options positions, the sensitivity of the position value to price changes (delta), volatility changes (vega), and time decay (theta). And roll risk — the risk that a paper position approaching expiry cannot be rolled forward at an acceptable price.

The trading book is the desk's truth. A trader's P&L is not what they think they've made — it's what the book says, marked to today's market prices, with every position they actually have, not the ones they wish they had.

What this means for you

ETRM system knowledge is explicitly valued in almost every commodity trading job description outside the front office. Understanding what a trading book is, what goes into it, and how positions are marked to market gives you the context to add value in scheduling, analytics, risk, finance, or technology roles — not just trading.
